Crime & Socioeconomic Factors in the United States

An interactive visual analysis of how economic inequality, unemployment, and GDP growth relate to property crime rates across 50 US states (2005–2015)

About this Dashboard

This page presents three interactive visualisations exploring whether economic conditions — such as GDP per capita, the Gini coefficient (income inequality), unemployment, and poverty — can help explain differences in property crime rates across US states. The dataset was sourced from Kaggle, originally compiled from FBI records and US Census Bureau income data.

Each chart below targets a specific research question. Use the interactions described in the panels to filter, highlight, and explore the data.

Hover / Tooltip Hover over any mark to see exact values for the state, indicator, and crime type.

Q1

Which states have the highest rates of a particular crime type — and which economic indicators explain this?

Grouped bar chart comparing socioeconomic indicators for the highest vs lowest crime state per crime type.

How to read: Each group of bars represents one crime type. Full-opacity bars are the highest-crime state; dimmed bars show the lowest-crime state. Use the dropdown to switch the socioeconomic indicator shown on the Y-axis. Hover any bar for the exact state name and value.
Crime type colours: Larceny Burglary Motor Vehicle Theft Robbery Full opacity = Highest crime state · Dimmed = Lowest crime state

💡 Key Insights

  • GDP per capita: Highest-crime states (California, Arkansas, Nevada) tend to have lower GDP than their lowest-crime counterparts (Vermont, Wyoming).
  • Poverty & Unemployment: Both track closely with crime — states with the highest crime have markedly higher poverty and unemployment rates.
  • Education ratio: California, Arkansas, and Nevada all have much higher proportions of people with fewer than 9 years of education compared to South Dakota, New York, and Vermont.
  • Corruption: No consistent pattern — South Dakota has higher corruption than California despite lower larceny, making corruption an unreliable predictor.
Q2

Which crime type is least / most associated with unemployment and the Gini coefficient?

Correlation heatmap linked to scatter plots — click a crime type in the heatmap to highlight it in both scatter plots.

How to read: The left heatmap shows the Pearson correlation between each crime type and two economic metrics. Red = strong positive correlation; Blue = negative correlation. Click any row in the heatmap to highlight that crime type in the two scatter plots on the right, letting you see the actual data points behind the correlation value. Hover any cell or point for exact values.

Key Insights

  • Robbery is the crime type most strongly correlated with both unemployment and the Gini coefficient — suggesting it is most sensitive to economic deprivation and inequality.
  • Larceny is the least associated with both metrics, indicating it may be driven more by opportunity than economic need.
  • The scatter plots confirm the heatmap: robbery data points show a clearer upward trend with both unemployment and Gini than burglary or larceny.
Q3

Does economic growth (GDP per capita increase) lead to lower crime rates — or is the effect offset by rising inequality?

Scatter plot of GDP change vs Gini change, coloured by economic trajectory and sized by average total crime rate.

How to read: Each point is one US state. The X-axis shows the change in GDP per capita from 2005 to 2015; the Y-axis shows the change in Gini coefficient (positive = more inequality). Colour and shape encode the economic scenario (four quadrants). Point size encodes the average total crime rate — larger = more crime. Scroll / drag to pan and zoom. Hover for state-level details.
Economic scenario: GDP↑ Inequality↓ (Best case) GDP↑ Inequality↑ (Growth but widening gap) GDP↓ Inequality↓ GDP↓ Inequality↑ (Worst case) Point size = avg total crime rate

Key Insights

  • The vast majority of states fall in the GDP↑ / Inequality↑ quadrant — economic growth between 2005–2015 was almost universally accompanied by rising income inequality.
  • States with GDP growth and rising inequality tend to have medium-to-high crime rates (larger points), showing that growth alone does not reduce crime.
  • Only one state achieved the ideal GDP↑ / Inequality↓ outcome — and it had the smallest average crime rate among growing states.
  • Policy implication: tackling inequality directly (minimum wage increases, targeted redistribution) may be more effective at reducing crime than GDP growth alone.

Data source: US State Crime & Socioeconomic Factors 2005–2015 on Kaggle , originally collected by the FBI (Uniform Crime Reports) and the US Census Bureau / Bureau of Economic Analysis.
